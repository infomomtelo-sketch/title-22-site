How to Open an RCFE in California

September 2026 · Licensing ICTP LIC 200 · Reviewed with a licensed RCFE administrator

Opening a residential care facility for the elderly is three projects wearing one hat: a property project, a personal certification project, and a records project that has to be finished before your first resident ever moves in. They run on different clocks, and the order matters more than the effort. The people who stall out are almost never the ones who worked less — they're the ones who started the 80-hour training after signing a lease, or found out about fire clearance after the building was painted.

This is the whole sequence, in the order that keeps the clocks from colliding: what you're applying for, how you become the certified administrator, and what new facilities get cited for in year one.

First, what you're actually applying for

An RCFE is licensed by the California Department of Social Services, Community Care Licensing Division, under the Residential Care Facilities for the Elderly Act (Health and Safety Code section 1569 and following) and Title 22, Division 6, Chapter 8 — the regulations that start at section 87100. The license authorizes non-medical care and supervision for persons 60 and over.

"Non-medical" is the load-bearing phrase, and every record requirement downstream follows from it. Your staff assist residents with the self-administration of medication; they do not practice nursing. Your facility keeps a needs and services plan; it does not keep a treatment plan. New operators who mentally file an RCFE as "a small nursing home" write the wrong policies and admit the wrong residents, and both show up in the first evaluation.

Step 1 — Decide your capacity before you sign anything

Licensed capacity is the decision that silently sets your budget, your building, and your timeline. Broadly, three bands behave very differently:

  • Six or fewer residents. Health and Safety Code section 1569.85 requires a facility of this size to be treated as a residential use of property for zoning purposes, so a city generally cannot force it into a conditional use permit or push it out of a residential neighborhood. This is why most first facilities are six-bed homes.
  • Seven to fifteen. You leave residential zoning treatment behind and start meeting commercial-side building and fire requirements. Different building, different budget, different conversation with the city.
  • Sixteen and up. Full commercial construction, fire and accessibility scope. A different business, not a bigger version of the first one.

Decide the band first, then look at property. Reversing those two is the single most expensive mistake in this list.

Step 2 — Get certified as an administrator

Every RCFE must be run by a certified administrator, and if you're opening your own facility, that person is usually you. The path is an 80-hour Initial Certification Training Program with a CDSS-approved vendor, then the state exam within 60 days of your last class day with a maximum of three attempts, then the LIC 9214 application packet within 30 days of passing. The certificate runs two years.

We wrote the whole path out separately — prerequisites, the exam rules, the fee, and the trap of scheduling the exam on day 55 — in ICTP requirements for new RCFE administrators. Two things worth saying here, in the context of opening a facility:

  • Start it first. The ICTP is the longest fixed-duration item on the board and nothing else depends on it, so it should be running while you're doing everything else. A lease with rent accruing while you wait for an exam date is money set on fire.
  • It's also your application vocabulary. The plan of operation asks for admission and retention policies, a staffing plan and a disaster plan. Those are Core of Knowledge topics. Doing the training first makes the packet take days instead of weeks.

Certification is not a one-time event, either: renewal takes 40 hours of continuing education every two years, split by rules that catch people out. That's the recertification checklist, and how to pick a CE vendor so the hours actually count.

Step 3 — Attend the mandatory CDSS orientation

California requires prospective applicants to attend a licensing orientation before applying — the requirement is in Health and Safety Code section 1569.23. It's given by the department, there's a fee, and you leave with the current application packet and the regional Adult and Senior Care office that will actually process your file. Take it after the ICTP if you can — the same material lands very differently once you know the regulations it's referring to.

The orientation is also where you get the current version of everything. Fees, forms and packet contents change; nothing you read online, this article included, outranks the checklist your regional office hands you.

Step 4 — Live Scan and criminal record clearances

Clearances are required for the licensee, the administrator, every employee with resident contact, and any adult living in the facility. Two details cause almost all of the pain:

  • Clearance comes before contact, not after. A caregiver working a shift while their clearance is pending is one of the fastest routes to a serious citation, and it is the deficiency analysts find most reliably, because it's provable from a file and a schedule.
  • A clearance has to be attached to your facility. An applicant who was cleared at a previous employer is not automatically cleared at yours — a transfer of the criminal record clearance has to be requested, under Title 22 section 87355(c). New administrators lose weeks to this because the person genuinely was cleared, somewhere else.

Step 5 — Property, zoning, and fire clearance

Own or lease, but if you lease, get the landlord's written consent to the use before you spend anything on the building. Then, in parallel: local business licensing, and a fire clearance from the fire authority having jurisdiction. The license does not issue without the fire clearance.

Fire clearance is the item most likely to move your opening date, and it's worth asking one question early: do you intend to admit non-ambulatory residents? That capability has to be cleared for specifically, and it changes what the building must have. Deciding it after clearance means doing clearance twice.

Step 6 — Write the plan of operation

The plan of operation is the part applicants underestimate, because it looks like paperwork and is actually the standard you'll be measured against for the life of the license. It typically covers the program statement, admission and retention policies, staffing and coverage, a sample admission agreement, the emergency disaster plan, food service and menus, and a facility sketch.

Write it as what you will really do. If your staffing plan says awake overnight staff, an analyst will look for awake overnight staff. If your retention policy says you keep residents through a described change of condition, you will be held to it. An aspirational plan of operation is a citation with a delay on it.

Step 7 — The application packet and the money behind it

The packet is built around the application for a license (LIC 200), plus the personnel, criminal record statement (LIC 508), health screening, organizational and financial forms your regional office specifies. Work from their current checklist rather than a list from the internet — form numbers and contents move.

The application fee is set by licensed capacity and is non-refundable. What sinks new facilities, though, isn't the fee — it's the gap between license issuance and census. You are paying rent, insurance, utilities and staff before residents arrive, and filling a six-bed home takes longer than the business plan assumed. Budget operating reserves for the empty months, not just the application. Health and Safety Code section 1569.605 also requires RCFEs to carry liability insurance covering injury to residents and guests — at least $1,000,000 per occurrence and $3,000,000 in total annual aggregate, in force since July 1, 2015. Confirm current limits with your broker before you quote a policy.

Step 8 — The pre-licensing inspection

Before the license issues, an analyst visits and walks the facility against your plan of operation and the regulations. Postings, the disaster plan, food storage, water temperature, locked chemicals, medication storage, exits, staffing.

The thing to understand about this visit is that it is a preview, not a test with a different syllabus. It's the same review you'll get unannounced, forever, starting the day you open. Which brings us to the part nobody plans for.

The first year: what actually gets cited

New facilities are rarely cited for anything exotic. They're cited for records that were true and are now late. Since July 2018, Health and Safety Code section 1569.33 has required the department to inspect every licensed RCFE unannounced at least once a year, and a complaint can bring an analyst any day in between. Here's where the deficiencies land:

  • Clearance before the first shift. Covered above; still the number one. Never let anyone work while it's pending.
  • Staff training hours, and their dates. Health and Safety Code section 1569.625 puts 40 hours of initial training on direct care staff — 20 before they work independently with residents, 20 within the first four weeks — then 20 hours a year after that, with dementia-care hours specified inside both totals. The hours are rarely the problem. The dates are: nobody can produce the certificate, or the annual came due in month 13.
  • A certified administrator, present. Certification lapses quietly. So does the coverage rule in Title 22 section 87405: the administrator has to be on the premises enough hours to actually run the place, and when they are not, a designated substitute qualified to be accountable for the facility has to be covering. "I was at the pharmacy" is not a coverage plan.
  • Resident records that describe today's resident. A complete admission set is the easy half — identification and emergency information (LIC 601), the physician's report (LIC 602A), preplacement appraisal (LIC 603), needs and services plan (LIC 625), signed personal rights, current admission agreement. The half that gets cited is reappraisal: after a fall, a hospitalization, or a real change of condition, the needs and services plan has to change too. Our admission paperwork guide walks the full set.
  • Incidents reported on the clock, with follow-through. Under Title 22 section 87211, unusual incidents are reported to licensing by telephone within 24 hours or the next working day, with the written report — the LIC 624 — following within seven days. Then the real question: did the care plan change? A reported fall with no reappraisal is the cross-reference analysts make first.
  • Medication documentation with no blanks. Centrally stored medication records matching what's in the med room, a MAR with a reason for every dose not taken, PRN documentation including the resident's response, and witnessed destruction records. The medication documentation checklist has the full record set and retention periods.
  • Postings and the disaster plan. License, personal rights, complaint information, the current disaster plan, posted menus that match what's served. These are the cheapest points on the board and they're missed constantly.
  • Correcting deficiencies by the date on the plan of correction. A deficiency written up with a correction date and left uncorrected stops being a paperwork issue and starts accruing civil penalties. Whatever else you do, meet the date.

The pattern underneath all of it

Read that list again and notice what almost none of it is. It isn't judgment, or care quality, or anything an experienced administrator doesn't already know. It's dated records: a clearance with a date, a training hour with a date, a certificate with an expiry, an incident with a reporting deadline, a correction with a due date. Every item is easy on the day it happens and nearly impossible to reconstruct eleven months later, which is exactly when someone asks for it.

Paper binders don't warn you. That's the whole problem — a binder is a place to put a date, not a thing that watches one. New facilities don't fail because the administrator didn't care; they fail because month 13 arrived without announcing itself.
